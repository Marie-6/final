How people learn to talk in a new language

What the research says about learning a language well enough for everyday conversation, and what actually makes it faster.

Main conclusion

Most people don't fail at conversation because they don't know enough. They fail because they don't speak enough, and fear is a big reason they don't.

The fastest route to daily conversation combines lots of understandable listening, the most common words and ready-made phrases reviewed spaced out over days, and real speaking with feedback from early on. Underneath all of it, the learner needs to feel safe enough to try. Apps are good at the memory part, but the research points to the same gap again and again: getting from knowing words to actually saying them out loud to a real person.

Understandable input+ Frequent words & chunks+ Spaced self-testing+ Speaking with feedback+ Low fear

What "daily conversation" means

On the CEFR scale (A1 → C2), everyday conversation is roughly A2–B1. That's far below full fluency.

LevelYou can…
A2Handle simple routine exchanges: ordering food, shopping, small talk about yourself.
B1Deal with most travel situations and talk about familiar topics, plans and experiences.

How hard is the language?

The US Foreign Service Institute estimates the classroom hours English speakers need for professional fluency. Daily conversation takes much less, but the ratio shows the difficulty.

Spanish, French, Italian
~600–750 h
Mandarin, Japanese, Korean, Arabic
~2,200 h

FSI figures come from their own intensive classes, not controlled studies. Use them as a rough guide only.

These aren't rival theories. Together they describe one loop: understand → notice → try to say it → get feedback → repeat.

The evidence, topic by topic

Tap a topic to open it.

Interaction is the strongest factor Strong
  • A major review (Loewen & Sato, 2018) concludes interaction promotes language development, though task design and who you talk to matter.
  • Adults usually reach speaking proficiency only after many hours of deliberate practice. Kids can pick it up from exposure; adults need to practice on purpose.
  • Motivation, interaction and vocabulary are named as the three most critical areas for adult learners (Center for Applied Linguistics).
Speak early and often. Conversation is the practice, not the reward at the end.
How many words do you need? Strong
95% coverage
2,000–3,000
98% coverage
5,000–7,000

Word families needed to cover informal spoken English. 95% is roughly the minimum to follow along; 98% is comfortable.

  • Studies of TV and soap operas: ~2,000 families for 95% (Al-Surmi, 2014); ~3,000 for movies and TV (Webb & Rodgers).
  • Real conversation may need a bit more, because speech goes by fast and you can't re-read it.
The first 1,000 words pay off the most. Learn words by how often they're used, not by themes like "zoo animals".
Chunks and ready-made phrases Medium
  • Native speakers rely heavily on pre-built phrases (Wray, 2002).
  • Learners who use more chunks are rated more fluent (Boers et al., 2006; François & Albakry, 2021).
  • But just noticing chunks while reading doesn't reliably carry over into speaking (Boers & Lindstromberg, 2012).
Learn phrases, then use them out loud. Seeing them isn't enough.
Memory: spacing and self-testing Strong
  • Retrieval practice (testing yourself) beats re-studying.
  • A meta-analysis of language spacing studies (Kim & Webb, 2022) found a reliable small-to-moderate benefit for spacing.
  • In general memory research, spaced retrieval beat massed retrieval with a large effect (g ≈ 0.74). Gradually expanding the gaps wasn't clearly better than equal gaps.
  • Most studies use flashcard word pairs. They show you can remember words, not that you can use them.
Flashcards are great for memory, but they're only one part of learning to talk.
Corrective feedback Strong
  • Feedback on errors works, with a medium effect that lasts over time (Li, 2010 meta-analysis).
  • Prompts (pushing the learner to self-correct) had larger effects than recasts (the teacher just says it correctly) in classroom studies (Lyster & Saito, 2010).
"Try that again?" teaches more than quietly fixing someone's sentence.
Shadowing for pronunciation and fluency Early
  • Shadowing means listening to native audio and repeating it at the same time, like an echo.
  • Several small studies report better fluency and pronunciation, but most are short, single-classroom studies.
Promising and cheap to try, but not yet proven at scale.
Speaking anxiety, the hidden blocker Medium
  • Foreign Language Classroom Anxiety (Horwitz, Horwitz & Cope, 1986) works like stage fright and is strongest during speaking.
  • Higher anxiety goes with lower Willingness to Communicate (MacIntyre). Anxious learners avoid speaking, practice less, and stay anxious.
Making speaking feel safer and lower-stakes speeds up learning indirectly.
Sleep Medium
  • Sleeping between study sessions meant learners needed less practice to relearn words, and they remembered them longer (Mazza et al., 2016).
  • A short nap helped new foreign words stick (University of Bern, 2019).
  • Data from the language app Memrise also suggests sleep helps memory outside the lab.
Review before bed, then sleep.

What about apps and AI?

Duolingo & apps Mixed

Duolingo's own 2021 study (258 learners) found about half reached A2 speaking: ~66% for Spanish, ~53% for French. The company called the gains "modest". Most of this research is written by Duolingo staff, and critics point to biased samples.

Good for vocabulary and daily habits. Weak on real conversation.

AI conversation partners Early

2025–2026 studies from several countries report AI speaking practice reduces nervousness and improves confidence and fluency. But most are small, short, or have no control group. Students and teachers agree AI can't fully replace human conversation.

Promising as a rehearsal space before talking to real people.

A research-based daily routine

About 40 minutes a day, plus one real conversation a week.

10 min
Spaced flashcards

The most common words and phrases. Test yourself; don't just read.

15 min
Listen

Podcasts, shows or YouTube slightly above your level.

5 min
Shadow

Repeat a short clip out loud at the same time as the speaker.

10 min
Speak

With a person, a tutor, or AI as rehearsal. Ask to be corrected.

Night
Review, then sleep

A quick review before bed helps it stick.

Weekly
One real conversation

A language exchange, tutor, café or friend.

Gaps in the research and design opportunities

Where the research shows a problem that hasn't been solved yet. These are starting points for my final project.

How might we make the first real conversations feel safe?Fear blocks practice, so lowering it speeds everything up.
How might we bridge practicing in an app and talking in real life?People recognize words in an app but freeze in a real café.
How might we get people to use phrases, not just see them?Reading chunks doesn't carry over into speaking.
How might feedback prompt learners to fix their own mistakes?Self-correction beats being corrected.
How might we make daily practice something people want to come back to?Spaced repetition works, but people quit.
How might we turn everyday moments into speaking practice?Adults need deliberate practice: cooking, commuting, ordering coffee.
