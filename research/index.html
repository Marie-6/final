Why people study a language for months and still can't order a coffee

Research for my final project · November 2026 · plain notes version

I wanted to know how adults learn a new language well enough for everyday conversation, and why so many of us get stuck. I read reviews, meta-analyses and app studies. This is what I found.

The main problem

Most learners don't get stuck because they know too little. They get stuck because they almost never practice speaking, and they're scared to.

People spend months on flashcards and apps, and they can recognize hundreds of words on a screen. Then a real person asks them a question and their mind goes blank.

That's because recognizing a word and saying it out loud in real time are different skills. Most study time goes into the first one, but conversation needs the second.

The gap between studying a language and speaking it.

Why it happens

  1. Adults need to practice speaking on purpose. Children can pick a language up just by being around it. Adults mostly don't. Research reviews keep finding that real back-and-forth conversation is what builds speaking skill.1,2
  2. Apps train memory more than speaking. Duolingo's own study found that about half of learners reached basic speaking level (A2) by the end of that section of the course, and the company called the speaking gains "modest".3 Most of this research is done by Duolingo's own staff.4
  3. Fear keeps people quiet. Language anxiety works like stage fright, and it's worst when speaking.5 Nervous learners are less willing to talk,6 so they get less practice and stay nervous:
Scared to speak → speak less → less practice → still unsure → scared to speak

What actually helps

The research agrees on a short list. None of these is a secret. The problem is that most people only do the first two.

Learn the most common words first

About 2,000 to 3,000 word families cover around 95% of everyday spoken English. That's roughly enough to follow a conversation.7 Learning by frequency works better than learning themed lists like "zoo animals".

Test yourself, spread out over days

Trying to remember a word works better than re-reading it, and reviewing over several days works better than cramming.8 Sleeping between sessions also helps words stick.9 One catch: most of these studies use flashcards, so they show people can remember words, not that they can use them.

Learn whole phrases, then say them

Fluent speakers lean on ready-made phrases like "Can I get…" and "What do you mean?". Learners who use more of these sound more fluent. But just reading phrases doesn't carry over into speaking. You have to actually use them.10

Get corrected, and try again yourself

Feedback on mistakes works. Being asked to fix your own mistake ("Sorry, you goed?") teaches more than just hearing someone say it correctly.11

Make speaking feel safe

Early studies of AI conversation partners mostly find one thing: they make people less nervous. Learners get a place to rehearse where nobody is judging them.12 These studies are small, and students still say AI can't replace a real person. That makes it a rehearsal, not the real thing.

What "daily conversation" means

On the standard language scale (A1 to C2), everyday conversation is around A2 to B1: ordering food, small talk, getting around while traveling, talking about familiar topics. It's a reachable goal, far below full fluency.

How long it takes depends on the language. The US Foreign Service Institute estimates about 600–750 class hours for an English speaker to become professionally fluent in Spanish or French, and about 2,200 for Mandarin, Japanese, Korean or Arabic.13 Daily conversation takes much less than that, but the ratio is similar.

Conclusion

Learning words is the part we've already figured out. Apps and flashcards handle it well. The hard part is the jump from knowing words to saying them to a real person, and fear is what stops people from making it.

So for my final project, I'm not going to design another way to memorize vocabulary. I want to design for that jump. Some questions I'm starting from:

  • How might we make someone's first real conversations feel less scary?
  • How might we help people actually say phrases out loud, not just read them?
  • How might practice push people to fix their own mistakes?
  • How might everyday moments, like cooking or ordering coffee, become speaking practice?
