# How People Learn a New Language (for Daily Conversation)

Research notes for my final project. The goal is to understand **how adults learn to hold everyday conversations in a new language, and what makes it faster**, before deciding what to design.

Last updated: Nov 17, 2026

---

## TL;DR: the 8 things that matter most

1. **Lots of understandable listening.** You learn mainly from input you *almost* fully understand.
2. **Speaking with real people (interaction).** Back-and-forth conversation, where you have to make yourself understood, is the best-supported driver of speaking progress.
3. **Learn the most common ~2,000–3,000 words first.** That covers about 95% of everyday spoken language.
4. **Learn chunks, not just single words.** "Can I get…", "What do you mean?", "No worries". Ready-made phrases make you sound fluent quickly.
5. **Test yourself, spaced out over days.** Recalling a word beats re-reading it, and spacing reviews beats cramming.
6. **Get corrected, ideally by being asked to fix it yourself.** Feedback works, and prompts ("Sorry, *I goed*?") work better than just hearing the correct version.
7. **Lower the fear.** Speaking anxiety is the hidden blocker. Anxious learners talk less, so they practice less.
8. **Sleep.** Sleep between study sessions helps memories stick.

## 1. What "daily conversation" actually requires

- On the **CEFR** scale (A1 → C2), handling everyday conversations is roughly **A2–B1**. A2 means simple routine exchanges (ordering, shopping, small talk). B1 means dealing with most travel situations and talking about familiar topics.
- **How long it takes depends on the language.** The US Foreign Service Institute (FSI) groups languages by the classroom hours English speakers need to reach *professional* fluency. Daily conversation needs much less than this, but the ratios show how hard each language is:

| FSI category | Examples | Approx. hours to professional level |
|---|---|---|
| I (easiest) | Spanish, French, Italian, Dutch | ~600–750 |
| V (hardest) | Mandarin, Cantonese, Japanese, Korean, Arabic | ~2,200 |

> FSI numbers come from their own teaching experience, not controlled studies, and they're for intensive classes with professional teachers. Treat them as a rough difficulty guide.

## 2. The big theories (in plain words)

| Theory | Who | Idea | What it means for learners |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Input hypothesis** | Krashen (1982) | We acquire language by understanding messages slightly above our level ("i+1"). | Listen and read a lot, at the right level. |
| **Output hypothesis** | Swain (1985) | Being forced to *speak or write* makes you notice gaps in what you know. | Input alone isn't enough. You need to produce. |
| **Interaction hypothesis** | Long (1996) | Conversation, especially when meaning breaks down and you fix it together, drives learning. | Talk with people and ask "what do you mean?" |
| **Noticing hypothesis** | Schmidt (1990) | You only learn what you consciously notice. | Pay attention to how natives *actually* say things. |
| **Four strands** | Paul Nation | A good program balances 4 equal parts: meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, deliberate language study, and fluency practice. | Don't only do flashcards, and don't only "chat". Do both. |

These ideas aren't rivals. Modern research treats them as **pieces of one process**: understand → notice → try to say it → get feedback → repeat.

## 3. Evidence, topic by topic

### 3.1 Interaction is the strongest factor
- A state-of-the-art review (Loewen & Sato, 2018, *Language Teaching*) concludes that interaction promotes L2 development, but how well it works depends on task design, who you talk to, and how learners are grouped.
- For adults especially, speaking proficiency seems to come from **many hours of deliberate practice**, not just exposure (Martin, 2019 overview). Kids can "pick it up". Adults usually need to practice on purpose.
- Motivation, interaction, and vocabulary knowledge are named as the three most critical areas for adult learners (CAELA / Center for Applied Linguistics).

### 3.2 Vocabulary: how many words do you need?
- About **2,000–3,000 word families** give around **95% coverage** of informal spoken English. 95% is roughly the minimum to follow what's going on.
- To reach **98%** (comfortable understanding), you need about **5,000–7,000** word families.
- Studies of TV and soap operas: ~2,000 families for 95% (Al-Surmi, 2014), ~3,000 for movies and TV (Webb & Rodgers).
- **Takeaway:** the first 1,000 words give the biggest payoff. Learn by frequency, not by themed lists like "zoo animals".

### 3.3 Chunks and formulaic language
- Native speakers rely on pre-built phrases (Wray, 2002, *Formulaic Language and the Lexicon*).
- Learners who use more formulaic sequences are rated as **more fluent and more proficient** (Boers et al., 2006; François & Albakry, 2021).
- **But:** just *noticing* chunks while reading doesn't reliably transfer to speaking. Learners need to practice using them (Boers & Lindstromberg, 2012).

### 3.4 Memory: spacing and retrieval
- **Retrieval practice** (testing yourself) beats re-studying.
- **Spacing** beats cramming. A meta-analysis of L2 spacing studies (Kim & Webb, 2022, *Language Learning*) found a reliable small-to-moderate benefit.
- In general memory research, spaced retrieval beat massed retrieval with a large effect (g ≈ 0.74). Interestingly, *expanding* intervals (1 day, 3 days, 7 days…) weren't clearly better than equal ones.
- **Caveat:** most studies use flashcard-style word pairs. They show you can *remember* words, not that you can *use* them in conversation.

### 3.5 Corrective feedback
- Feedback on errors works, with a **medium overall effect that lasts over time** (Li, 2010 meta-analysis, *Language Learning*).
- **Prompts** (pushing the learner to self-correct) had larger effects than **recasts** (the teacher just repeats the correct form) in classroom studies (Lyster & Saito, 2010).
- **Takeaway:** "Try that again?" teaches more than silently fixing someone's sentence.

### 3.6 Pronunciation and fluency: shadowing
- **Shadowing** means listening to native audio and repeating it at the same time, like an echo.
- Several small studies report gains in fluency and pronunciation. The evidence is promising but thin: mostly short, single-classroom studies.

### 3.7 Anxiety is the hidden blocker
- **Foreign Language Classroom Anxiety** (Horwitz, Horwitz & Cope, 1986) is a specific kind of anxiety, like stage fright, and it's strongest during **speaking**.
- Higher anxiety is linked to lower **Willingness to Communicate** (MacIntyre). Anxious learners avoid speaking, so they practice less, which keeps them anxious. It's a loop.
- **Design insight:** anything that makes speaking feel safer and lower stakes can speed up learning indirectly.

### 3.8 Sleep
- Sleeping between study sessions reduced the practice needed to relearn words and improved long-term retention (Mazza et al., 2016, *Psychological Science*).
- A short nap helped consolidate new foreign words (University of Bern, *Current Biology*, 2019).
- Real-world app data (Memrise) also points to sleep-related consolidation outside the lab.

## 4. What about apps and AI? (current state, 2026)

### Duolingo and other apps
- Duolingo's own study (2021, 258 learners) found roughly **half reached A2 speaking** by the end of its A2 section (~66% for Spanish, ~53% for French). The company called its speaking gains "modest".
- Most Duolingo research is written by Duolingo employees, and critics point to sample bias, since most participants had university degrees.
- **Takeaway:** apps are good for vocabulary and habits, but weak on real conversation.

### AI conversation partners (chatbots, voice AI)
- A wave of 2025–2026 studies (Georgia, Iraq, Indonesia and other places) reports that AI speaking practice **reduces speaking anxiety** and improves **confidence and fluency**.
- **But** most are small, short, or lack a control group. Students and teachers agree AI **can't fully replace** human conversation, which feels more authentic.
- **Takeaway:** AI is promising as a *rehearsal space*, a low-pressure place to practice before talking to real people.

## 5. A research-based learning routine (for daily conversation)

| Daily | What | Why (section) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 min | Spaced flashcards of the **top-frequency words and chunks** | 3.2, 3.3, 3.4 |
| 15 min | **Listen** to something slightly above your level (podcasts, shows, YouTube) | 2 (Input) |
| 5 min | **Shadow** a short clip out loud | 3.6 |
| 10 min | **Speak**: with a person, a tutor, or AI as rehearsal. Ask for corrections. | 3.1, 3.5, 4 |
| Night | Review before sleep, then **sleep** | 3.8 |

Weekly: one real conversation with a human (language exchange, tutor, café, friend).

## 6. Open questions and design opportunities (for my project)

These are the **gaps** the research points to. Good places for a design intervention:

1. **Fear of speaking.** How might we make the first real conversations feel safe? (3.7)
2. **The jump from app to real life.** People can recognize words in an app but freeze in a real café. How might we bridge rehearsal and real situations? (4)
3. **Chunks don't transfer by just reading them.** How might we get people to *use* phrases, not just see them? (3.3)
4. **Self-correction beats being corrected.** How might feedback prompt learners to fix their own mistakes? (3.5)
5. **Practice is boring.** Spaced repetition works, but people quit. How might we make daily practice something people want to come back to? (3.4)
6. **Adults need deliberate practice.** How might we turn everyday moments (cooking, commuting, ordering coffee) into speaking practice? (3.1)
